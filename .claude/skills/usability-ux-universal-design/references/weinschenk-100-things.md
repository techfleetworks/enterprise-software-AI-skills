# 100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People

A working summary, in our own words, of the 100 research-backed usability findings in Susan Weinschenk's
*100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People* (New Riders, 2011, ISBN 978-0-321-76753-0), each
with the design takeaway it implies. This is a paraphrase for day-to-day use, not a reproduction; read
the book for the full research and examples. Findings are grouped by the book's ten themes.

## How People See

### 1. What you see is not what your brain gets
The brain constantly interprets and fills in what the eyes take in, using shortcuts built from past experience, so what a person perceives is not a raw copy of what is on the screen.
**Design takeaways:**
- What you expect people to notice on a page may not match what they actually notice, because it depends on their background, knowledge, familiarity, and expectations.
- You can sometimes nudge people to perceive things a certain way through how you present them.

### 2. Peripheral vision is used more than central vision to get the gist of what you see
Central vision handles detail, but people rely on peripheral vision to understand the overall type of scene they are looking at.
**Design takeaways:**
- People use peripheral vision at a screen and often judge what a page is about from a quick glimpse of the edges.
- The center matters for detail, but do not neglect the periphery; make sure the outer areas clearly signal what the page and the whole site are for.
- If you want people to concentrate on one part of the screen, keep animation and blinking out of their peripheral vision.

### 3. People identify objects by recognizing patterns
The eyes and brain look for patterns even where none exist, and people recognize objects by breaking them into simple geometric shapes.
**Design takeaways:**
- Use patterns wherever you can, since people are already looking for them; grouping and white space help create them.
- To make an object or icon easy to recognize, draw it as a simple geometric shape so its basic forms read quickly.
- Favor flat 2D elements over 3D ones, since the eyes pass a 2D image to the brain and 3D can slow recognition and understanding.

### 4. There is a special part of the brain just for recognizing faces
A dedicated brain region recognizes faces faster than ordinary objects and sits close to the brain's emotional center.
**Design takeaways:**
- People spot and react to faces on a page faster than to anything else (except for people who are autistic).
- A face looking straight at the viewer has the strongest emotional pull, likely because the eyes carry more weight than any other facial feature.
- When a pictured face looks toward a product or spot on the page, people tend to look there too, though looking is not the same as paying attention.

### 5. People imagine objects tilted and at a slight angle above
People picture, remember, and recognize objects fastest from a canonical view, slightly above and turned a little to one side.
**Design takeaways:**
- A drawing or object is recognized faster and remembered better when shown from this canonical viewpoint.
- Draw the icons in your site or application from a canonical perspective.

### 6. People scan screens based on past experience and expectations
Where people look depends on their reading direction, their mental models, and their expectations, and they tend to skip the edges and move toward the center and meaningful content.
**Design takeaways:**
- Place the most important content, along with anything meant to catch the eye, in the top third or the center of the screen.
- Avoid placing anything important at the edges, where people tend not to look.
- Lay out the screen so people can move in their normal reading pattern, and avoid forcing them to jump back and forth across the screen to finish a task.

### 7. People see cues that tell them what to do with an object
Objects give off cues (affordances) about how to handle them, and people figure out what to do by reading those perceived cues.
**Design takeaways:**
- Design in affordance cues; giving people clear hints about what they can do with an object makes them more likely to act.
- Use shading to show when something is selected or active.
- Do not give misleading cues that suggest the wrong action.
- Rethink cues that depend on hovering if you are designing for a touch device instead of a pointer.

### 8. People can miss changes in their visual fields
People often fail to notice even large changes in what they see, especially when they are focused on one thing and are not expecting a change.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not assume people will see something just because it is on screen; this is especially risky when you refresh a page and alter one thing, since users may not realize the screen even changed.
- To be sure a change is noticed, add an extra visual cue such as blinking or an auditory cue such as a beep.
- Interpret eye-tracking data with caution, and do not lean on it as the main basis for design choices.

### 9. People believe that things that are close together belong together
When items sit near each other, people assume they are related, and this grouping is strongest for items placed side by side.
**Design takeaways:**
- To make pictures, headings, or text read as a group, place them close together.
- Before adding lines or boxes to separate or group items, first try changing the spacing, which is often enough and cuts visual clutter.
- Leave more space between items that do not belong together and less between items that do, a simple idea that many layouts ignore.

### 10. Red and blue together are hard on the eyes
Certain color pairings, especially red and blue, create a depth illusion that is tiring to look at or read.
**Design takeaways:**
- Avoid placing red and blue, or red and green, close together on a screen or page.
- Do not pair blue or green lettering with a red background, or red or green lettering with a blue background.

### 11. Nine percent of men and one-half percent of women are color-blind
Many people, mostly men, have a color deficiency (commonly red-green) that makes some colors hard to tell apart.
**Design takeaways:**
- Check your images and site with a color-blindness simulation tool to see how they look to someone with a color deficiency.
- When color carries meaning, add a second cue so the meaning does not depend on color alone, such as color plus a surrounding box.
- When building color coding, pick colors that work for everyone, such as shades of brown and yellow, and avoid relying on red, green, and blue.

### 12. The meanings of colors vary by culture
Colors carry associations and meanings that differ from one culture or subgroup to another.
**Design takeaways:**
- Choose colors deliberately, with their likely meanings in mind.
- Identify the main cultures or countries you are designing for and check your colors against a cultural color reference to avoid unintended associations.

## How People Read

### 13. It is a myth that capital letters are inherently hard to read
All-capital text is read slightly slower only because people see it less often, not because of word shape, since reading actually works by recognizing letters.
**Design takeaways:**
- People read all caps as shouting and are not used to it, so use all uppercase sparingly.
- Reserve all capitals for headlines or for moments when you need to grab attention, such as before deleting an important file.

### 14. Reading and comprehending are two different things
Being able to read the words does not mean understanding them; comprehension depends on prior knowledge, point of view, and context.
**Design takeaways:**
- People are active readers, so what they understand and remember depends on their experience, their viewpoint while reading, and the instructions they got beforehand.
- Do not assume people will remember specific details from what they read.
- Give content a meaningful title or headline, which is one of the most valuable things you can do.
- Match the reading level to your audience by using simple words and fewer syllables so more people can follow it.

### 15. Pattern recognition helps people identify letters in different fonts
People recognize letters through learned patterns, so serif versus sans serif makes no real difference, but overly decorative fonts break the pattern and slow reading.
**Design takeaways:**
- Serif and sans serif fonts are equal for readability.
- Unusual or heavily decorative fonts can disrupt pattern recognition and slow reading down.
- If a font is hard to read, people transfer that sense of difficulty to the content itself and decide the subject is hard to do or understand.

### 16. Font size matters
Text must be large enough to read without strain, and a font's x-height can make two same-size fonts look like different sizes.
**Design takeaways:**
- Pick a point size large enough for people of many ages to read comfortably.
- Choose a font with a large x-height for on-screen reading so the type appears bigger.

### 17. Reading a computer screen is harder than reading paper
Screens constantly refresh and emit light, which tires the eyes, unlike the stable, reflected light of paper or electronic ink.
**Design takeaways:**
- Use a large point size for on-screen text to reduce eye strain.
- Break text into chunks with bullets, short paragraphs, and pictures.
- Give plenty of contrast between text and background, with black text on white being the most readable.
- Make sure the content is worth reading, since in the end it comes down to whether the text interests your audience.

### 18. People read faster with a longer line length, but they prefer a shorter line length
Longer lines of about 100 characters are read fastest on screen, yet people prefer, and believe they read faster with, shorter lines and multiple columns.
**Design takeaways:**
- This is a trade-off: give people the short lines and multiple columns they prefer, or the longer single column they actually read faster with.
- Use a longer line of around 100 characters when reading speed matters.
- Use a shorter line of about 45 to 72 characters when reading speed is less important.
- For a multipage article, consider multiple columns with a short line of about 45 characters.

## How People Remember

### 19. Short-term memory is limited
Working memory holds very little, is easily disrupted by other input, and depends on keeping your attention focused on what you want to hold.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not make people carry information from one place to another, such as reading numbers on one page and typing them on the next, or they will likely forget and get frustrated.
- When you ask people to hold something in working memory, do not ask them to do anything else until they finish, since extra input breaks their focus.

### 20. People remember only four items at once
The real working-memory limit is about four items, not seven, and grouping or chunking lets people handle more.
**Design takeaways:**
- Holding people to four items is ideal but not required; you can present more as long as you group and chunk it.
- Keep each chunk to no more than four items.
- Remember that people lean on external aids like notes, lists, and calendars so they do not have to rely on memory.

### 21. People have to use information to make it stick
Information moves into long-term memory through repetition or by connecting it to something the person already knows (a schema).
**Design takeaways:**
- To help people remember something, repeat it again and again, because practice really does help.
- A key reason to run user research is to discover and understand the schemas your audience already holds.
- When your information relates to a schema people already have, point that connection out so it is easier to learn and recall.

### 22. It is easier to recognize information than recall it
Recognition draws on context and is far easier than recall, though the schemas that help recall can also cause people to "remember" things that were not there.
**Design takeaways:**
- Reduce memory load wherever you can, which is what many interface conventions were created to do.
- Avoid making people recall information, since recognizing it is much easier than pulling it from memory.

### 23. Memory takes a lot of mental resources
Thinking about, encoding, and storing information uses heavy mental resources, and memory is easily disrupted, with the start and end of an experience remembered best.
**Design takeaways:**
- Use concrete words and icons, which are easier to remember.
- Let people rest, and even sleep, when you want them to retain information.
- Try not to interrupt people while they are learning or encoding something.
- Expect material in the middle of a presentation to be the least remembered.

### 24. People reconstruct memories each time they remember them
Memories are rebuilt every time they are recalled, so they shift over time and can be reshaped or even invented by later events and by wording.
**Design takeaways:**
- When you test or interview customers about a product, your choice of words can strongly color what they "remember."
- Do not trust self-reports of past behavior, because people will not accurately recall what they or others did or said.
- Treat what people say after the fact, such as recalling using your product or a support call, with a healthy dose of skepticism.

### 25. It is a good thing that people forget
Forgetting is adaptive, not a flaw, because the brain constantly and mostly unconsciously decides what to keep and what to drop so you can function.
**Design takeaways:**
- People will always forget some things.
- What gets forgotten is not something a person chooses.
- Design with forgetting in mind: when something truly matters, do not count on memory, but build it into the design or make it easy to find.

### 26. The most vivid memories are wrong
Flashbulb memories of dramatic events feel sharp and certain, but research shows they are often riddled with errors.
**Design takeaways:**
- When someone has had a dramatic or traumatic experience, keep two things in mind: they will be sure their memory is accurate, and it is probably not exactly accurate.

## How People Think

### 27. People process information better in bite-sized chunks
The conscious mind handles only a small amount at a time, so showing everything at once overwhelms people, while progressive disclosure reveals information as it is needed.
**Design takeaways:**
- Use progressive disclosure to show people what they need when they need it, with links to reach more.
- When trading off clicks against thinking, choose more clicks and less thinking.
- Before using progressive disclosure, do the research so you learn what most people are after and at what point.

### 28. Some types of mental processing are more challenging than others
Tasks place three kinds of load on people (cognitive, visual, and motor), and cognitive load is the most costly while motor load is the least.
**Design takeaways:**
- Review an existing product's loads to see whether reducing one or more would make it easier to use.
- Remember that asking people to think or remember (cognitive load) consumes the most mental resources.
- Look for trade-offs, such as cutting a cognitive load by adding a bit of visual or motor load.
- Make targets large enough to reach easily.

### 29. Minds wander 30 percent of the time
People's minds drift off task often, about 30 percent of the time and much more in some situations, which can help creativity but also cause them to miss things.
**Design takeaways:**
- People focus for only a limited time, so assume their minds are wandering often.
- Where it fits, use hyperlinks to support quick topic switching, which is part of why people enjoy browsing the web.
- Build in feedback about where people are, so that when they wander they can easily return or move forward.

### 30. The more uncertain people are, the more they defend their ideas
Conflicting ideas cause an uncomfortable tension (cognitive dissonance), and when people are not forced to change they tend to deny the opposing information, arguing even harder when they feel less certain.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not spend much effort trying to change someone's deeply held beliefs.
- The best way to shift a belief is to get the person to commit to something very small first.
- Do not just show people that their belief is illogical or wrong, because that can backfire and make them dig in harder.

### 31. People create mental models
People form a model in their minds of how something works, built from prior experience and assumptions, often before they ever use it.
**Design takeaways:**
- People always arrive with a mental model.
- Those mental models come from past experience.
- Not everyone holds the same mental model.
- A major reason to do user research is to understand your audience's mental models.

### 32. People interact with conceptual models
The conceptual model is what the design and interface actually present, and trouble arises when it does not match the user's mental model.
**Design takeaways:**
- Design the conceptual model on purpose rather than letting it bubble up from the underlying technology.
- The key to an intuitive experience is making the product's conceptual model match the audience's mental models as closely as possible.
- If a brand-new product cannot match anyone's mental model, provide training to help people build a new one.

### 33. People process information best in story form
Stories grab and hold attention, help people absorb information, and lead them to assume cause and effect.
**Design takeaways:**
- Stories are the natural way people take in information.
- Use a story when you want people to make a causal connection.
- No matter how dry you think your information is, stories make it clearer, more interesting, and more memorable.

### 34. People learn best from examples
People learn faster when shown concrete examples such as pictures, screenshots, and video rather than only being told what to do.
**Design takeaways:**
- People learn best by example, so show them rather than just telling them.
- Use pictures and screenshots to demonstrate by example.
- Better still, use short videos as examples.

### 35. People are driven to create categories
People naturally sort things into categories, and when faced with a lot of uncategorized information they will organize it themselves.
**Design takeaways:**
- People like to put things into categories.
- When a lot of information is not organized, people feel overwhelmed and start categorizing it on their own.
- Organize information for your audience as much as you can, keeping the four-item limit in mind.
- Gathering input on what groupings make sense is useful, but the critical thing is that you organize it, and what you name things often matters more than how you group them.
- If you are designing for children under about seven, any categories you build are really for the adults in that child's world, not the child.

### 36. Time is relative
The sense of how much time has passed is subjective, and more mental effort or more unpredictability makes a wait feel longer.
**Design takeaways:**
- Always show progress indicators so people know how long something will take.
- Where possible, keep the time a task takes consistent so people can set their expectations.
- To make a process feel shorter, break it into steps and reduce thinking, since mental effort is what makes time drag.

### 37. There are four ways to be creative
Creativity comes in four types, formed by crossing deliberate versus spontaneous with cognitive versus emotional, and each needs different conditions.
**Design takeaways:**
- Since there are different kinds of creativity, decide which type you are after before designing an experience to encourage it.
- Deliberate cognitive creativity needs deep knowledge and time, so supply the needed background, point to resources, and allow enough time.
- Deliberate emotional creativity needs quiet reflection, so you can offer prompts to ponder but should expect people to step offline for insight and then return to share.
- Spontaneous cognitive creativity needs a break from the problem, so set it up in one stage and have people come back later with a solution.
- Spontaneous emotional creativity probably cannot be designed for.
- Remember that your own design creativity follows the same rules, so give yourself time and sleep on a problem when you are stuck.

### 38. People can be in a flow state
In a flow state people are completely absorbed in a task, with focused attention, a clear and achievable goal, steady feedback, and a sense of control.
**Design takeaways:**
- Let people steer their own actions throughout the activity.
- Break the difficulty into stages so each goal feels challenging yet reachable.
- Provide constant feedback.
- Minimize distractions.

### 39. Culture affects how people think
Culture shapes thinking, so Westerners tend to focus on a main foreground object while East Asians attend more to context and background.
**Design takeaways:**
- People from different regions respond differently to photos and designs, with East Asians noticing and remembering context and background more than Westerners do.
- If you design for several cultures and regions, do audience research across those locations.
- When you read psychology research, be cautious about generalizing results that came from participants in just one region, and avoid overgeneralizing.

## How People Focus Their Attention

### 40. Attention is selective
People can lock onto one thing and filter out everything else, a skill that works both consciously and unconsciously.
**Design takeaways:**
- People will focus on just one thing and ignore the rest, as long as you give clear instructions and the task does not run too long.
- The unconscious is always scanning for certain things, including a person's own name and anything about food, sex, or danger.

### 41. People filter information
People seek out and notice information that confirms what they already believe and tend to ignore or discount what does not, which is efficient but can lead to bad choices.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not assume people will pay attention to the information you provide.
- Do not make assumptions, since something plain to you as the designer may be unclear to the people using your work.
- If people may be filtering, lean on color, size, motion, video, and sound to highlight what matters.
- When it is critical that people notice something, make it stand out far more than you think is necessary.

### 42. Well-practiced skills do not require conscious attention
Once a skill is practiced enough to become automatic, people can do it with almost no conscious attention, which is efficient but can also cause slip-ups.
**Design takeaways:**
- When people repeat a series of steps often, the action becomes automatic.
- If you require a repeated sequence, make it easy, but accept the trade-off that people may err because they stop paying attention.
- Make it easy to undo not only the last action but a whole sequence.
- Instead of making people repeat a task, consider letting them select all the items first and then act on them at once.

### 43. Expectations of frequency affect attention
People build an unconscious sense of how often something happens and often miss it when it occurs more or less than they expect.
**Design takeaways:**
- People form an unconscious mental model of how frequently an event occurs.
- When people must catch a rare but important event, use a strong signal to grab their attention when it happens.

### 44. Sustained attention lasts about ten minutes
People can hold attention on one task for only about seven to ten minutes before it starts to fade.
**Design takeaways:**
- Assume you have at most seven to ten minutes of a person's attention.
- To hold attention longer, introduce new information or a break.
- Keep online demos and tutorials under about seven minutes.

### 45. People pay attention only to salient cues
People notice only the cues that matter for their current task (the salient ones) and tune out the rest, even on familiar objects.
**Design takeaways:**
- Decide which cues are salient for your audience.
- Design so those salient cues are obvious.
- Accept that people will probably attend only to the salient cues.

### 46. People cannot actually multitask
People handle one mental task at a time and only switch between them quickly, so true multitasking does not exist except for a well-practiced physical task paired with a mental one.
**Design takeaways:**
- People will claim they can multitask, but they cannot.
- The people who brag most about multitasking tend to be the worst at it.
- Being young does not make someone a better multitasker.
- Do not force people to do two things at once, such as talking with a customer while completing a form, and if they must, give extra care to that form's usability.
- When you require multitasking, expect many errors and provide ways to fix them afterward.
- Holding a phone conversation at the wheel is as dangerous as driving drunk.

### 47. Danger, food, sex, movement, faces, and stories get the most attention
The survival-focused old brain cannot help noticing movement, faces, food, sex, danger, and stories.
**Design takeaways:**
- Food, sex, or danger will not suit every design, but wherever they fit they pull a great deal of attention.
- Use close-up images of faces.
- Work in stories wherever you can, even for material you think of as purely factual.

### 48. Loud noises startle and get attention
Sounds can seize attention, but people habituate to a signal they hear over and over and eventually stop noticing it.
**Design takeaways:**
- If you design an application, you may control the sounds tied to actions like making a mistake, reaching a goal, or donating.
- Match the sound to how much attention you need, saving high-attention sounds for moments that truly matter, such as before an action that cannot be undone.
- When you use sounds for attention, consider varying them so people do not habituate and the sounds keep working.

### 49. For people to pay attention to something, they must first perceive it
A stimulus has to be perceived before it can get attention, and whether people detect it depends on more than its presence, which signal detection theory describes as hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections.
**Design takeaways:**
- For a given task, think through the four outcomes of the signal detection chart and ask whether a false alarm or a miss would be more damaging.
- Adjust your design accordingly: tone down the signal when a false alarm is worse, and strengthen it when a miss is worse.

## What Motivates People

### 50. People are more motivated as they get closer to a goal
People speed up their effort as they near a goal (the goal-gradient effect), and even a false sense of early progress produces the same push.
**Design takeaways:**
- The shorter the remaining distance to a goal, the more motivated people are, especially once the end is in sight.
- You can create this boost with the illusion of progress, as with a card that starts with a few boxes pre-stamped, since it feels like progress even when the real requirement is unchanged.
- People enjoy being in a reward program, and reward members tend to be friendlier, chat longer, thank staff more, and tip more than non-members.
- Motivation and spending drop sharply right after a reward is earned, so a second reward level will not be very motivating at first.
- You are most likely to lose a customer right after they reach a reward.

### 51. Variable rewards are powerful
Reinforcement schedules shape how often people repeat a behavior, and a variable ratio schedule, where the payoff comes after an unpredictable number of tries, drives the most repetition.
**Design takeaways:**
- For a reward to work, it must be something that particular audience actually wants, so figure out what they truly value.
- Decide what behavior pattern you want and match the reward schedule to it, using a variable ratio schedule for the most repeated behavior.

### 52. Dopamine makes people addicted to seeking information
The dopamine system drives wanting and seeking rather than pleasure itself, which keeps people hunting for more information.
**Design takeaways:**
- People are driven to keep seeking information.
- The easier you make information to find, the more information-seeking people will do.

### 53. Unpredictability keeps people searching
Unpredictable rewards and the cues that signal them strongly stimulate the dopamine system, and small bits of information are especially addictive.
**Design takeaways:**
- Pairing cues such as sounds with incoming information drives people to seek more.
- Delivering small bits of information with a way to get more produces still more seeking.
- The less predictable the arrival of information, the more addictive seeking it becomes.

### 54. People are more motivated by intrinsic rewards than extrinsic rewards
Expected external rewards can actually reduce a desired behavior once the reward stops, while internal motivation sustains it, especially for open-ended work.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not assume money or another external reward is the best motivator, and look for intrinsic rewards instead.
- If you do give an external reward, it motivates more when it is unexpected.
- If your product lets people connect with others, that alone will motivate them to use it.

### 55. People are motivated by progress, mastery, and control
People are strongly driven by the feeling of making progress and gaining mastery, even without any pay.
**Design takeaways:**
- To build loyalty and repeat visits, offer activities people inherently want to do, such as connecting with friends or mastering a skill, not just paid tasks.
- When a task is boring, help motivate people by admitting it is dull and letting them tackle it their own way.
- Find ways to help people set goals and follow their own progress.
- Show people how far along they are toward each goal.

### 56. People's ability to delay gratification (or not) starts young
Whether a person can delay gratification shows up in early childhood and tends to carry on into adulthood.
**Design takeaways:**
- Some people can hold out for a later reward, and others cannot.
- People who struggle to delay gratification are more open to scarcity messages such as "only three left" or "available only until the end of the month."

### 57. People are inherently lazy
People do the least work they can to finish a task, choosing an adequate "good enough" option over the optimal one, a strategy called satisficing.
**Design takeaways:**
- Assume people will get things done with the least effort possible, which holds true more often than not.
- Expect people to satisfice, settling for the good-enough solution rather than the best one.

### 58. People will look for shortcuts only if the shortcuts are easy
People seek faster ways to do repeated tasks, but only when the shortcut or default is easy to find and use; otherwise they stick with old habits.
**Design takeaways:**
- Offer shortcuts, but only when they are easy to find, learn, and use, and even then do not assume people will use them.
- Supply defaults when you can predict what most people will usually want, and when choosing a wrong one by mistake will not cause costly errors.

### 59. People assume it is you, not the situation
People tend to explain others' behavior by personality (the fundamental attribution error) while explaining their own behavior by the situation.
**Design takeaways:**
- When interviewing people about using your product, be careful in your analysis, since you will tend to attribute behavior to personality and overlook situational factors.
- When a subject-matter or domain expert tells you what people do, weigh it carefully, because the expert may miss situational factors and overvalue personality.
- Build in ways to check your own biases, pausing before you act to ask whether you are making a fundamental attribution error.

### 60. Forming a habit takes a long time and requires small steps
Habits form slowly, sometimes over months, take longer for more complex behaviors, and depend on consistency.
**Design takeaways:**
- Hand people a small, easy task instead of a complex one.
- Offer people a reason to return and repeat it most days.
- Be patient, because forming a habit can take a long time.

### 61. People are more motivated to compete when there are fewer competitors
People try harder and perform better when they believe they are competing against a small number of others (the N effect).
**Design takeaways:**
- Competition can motivate, but do not overdo it.
- Showing more than about ten competitors can weaken the drive to compete.

### 62. People are motivated by autonomy
People like doing things themselves, their own way and on their own schedule, because autonomy gives them a sense of control.
**Design takeaways:**
- People like to do things for themselves and are motivated to do so.
- To encourage self-service, frame your messaging around having control and being able to do it yourself.

## People Are Social Animals

### 63. The strong tie group size limit is 150 people
Humans can maintain only about 150 stable, strong relationships (Dunbar's number), so large online networks are mostly made of weaker ties.
**Design takeaways:**
- There is a limit of roughly 150 people for a close survival community, and without that tribe people may feel alienated, isolated, and stressed.
- The many people you reach through social media are usually weak ties.
- When building social connections into a product, consider whether the interactions are for strong or weak ties.
- For strong ties, build in some physical proximity and let people in the network interact and come to know each other.
- For weak ties, do not depend on everyone communicating directly or being physically close.

### 64. People are hard-wired for imitation and empathy
Mirror neurons fire both when a person acts and when they watch someone else act, which underlies imitation and empathy.
**Design takeaways:**
- Do not underestimate the power of watching someone do something, so to influence a behavior, show another person doing that same task.
- Because stories create mental images that can also trigger mirror neurons, use stories when you want people to take an action.
- Video is especially compelling online, so to prompt an action, show others doing it, such as people getting a flu shot or kids eating vegetables.

### 65. Doing things together bonds people together
Doing the same thing at the same time in close proximity (synchronous activity) strengthens bonding and cooperation.
**Design takeaways:**
- Most online interaction is asynchronous, including most social media, which meets other needs but does not satisfy the desire for synchronous activity.
- Because most online interaction is not in physical proximity, chances to build in synchronous activity are limited.
- Look for ways to add synchronous activity to your product, such as a live-streamed video or a real-time audio or video link.

### 66. People expect online interactions to follow social rules
People treat going to a site or using an application as a social interaction and expect it to follow the same rules as a person-to-person exchange.
**Design takeaways:**
- When designing a product, consider the interactions a person will have with it, and whether they match the norms of a real exchange between two people.
- Many usability guidelines are really about social expectations, so following basic usability guidelines helps you meet those expectations.

### 67. People lie to differing degrees depending on the media
How honest people are varies by medium, with the most lying on the phone and the least with pen and paper, and people are also harsher over email.
**Design takeaways:**
- People are most dishonest on the phone and most truthful with pen and paper.
- People come across as harsher toward others in email than on paper.
- If you run email surveys, expect more negative responses than you would get on paper.
- For surveys or feedback, telephone surveys will not be as accurate as email or pen-and-paper ones.
- Customer and audience feedback is most accurate gathered in person, one-on-one.

### 68. Speakers' brains and listeners' brains sync up during communication
When a listener understands a speaker, their brain activity begins to mirror the speaker's, and this coupling depends on understanding and anticipation.
**Design takeaways:**
- Listening to someone talk creates a brain syncing that helps people grasp what is being said.
- Presenting information through audio or video, where people hear someone speak, is a powerful way to aid understanding.
- Do not rely on reading alone when you want people to understand the message clearly.

### 69. The brain responds uniquely to people you know personally
A specific brain region activates for friends and relatives but not for strangers you merely share interests with.
**Design takeaways:**
- Not all social media are alike, so it can matter whether a service is for friends and relatives or for people you are not yet connected to.
- People are wired to pay special attention to friends and relatives, so social media built around them are more motivating and earn more loyalty.
- People check a friends-and-family site far more often than a professional network, because the first is about close relationships.

### 70. Laughter bonds people together
Laughter is instinctive and mostly social rather than a response to jokes, and it creates bonding between people.
**Design takeaways:**
- Most online interaction is asynchronous and offers little chance for bonding through laughter.
- Synchronous online communication should create more bonding if it leaves room for laughter.
- You do not need humor or jokes to make people laugh, since ordinary conversation produces more laughter than deliberate joking.
- If you want people to laugh, laugh yourself, because laughter is contagious.

### 71. People can tell when a smile is real or fake more accurately with video
A fake smile can fool people in a still photo, but video makes real versus fake easier to spot through timing and flickers of other emotions.
**Design takeaways:**
- Pay attention to smiles in video, since people judge a real smile from a fake one better in video than in a photo, and a smile read as fake lowers trust.
- It is possible to fake a smile, even one with crinkled eyes, but faking is easier in a still picture than in video.
- People detect a real smile by watching for conflicting emotions across the whole face, not just the eyes.
- A smile that reads as genuine engages the viewer and builds trust.

## How People Feel

### 72. Seven basic emotions are universal
Seven emotions appear to be universal across cultures (joy, sadness, contempt, fear, disgust, surprise, and anger) and are shown through facial expressions.
**Design takeaways:**
- The seven basic emotions are universal and are conveyed by facial expression and physical gesture.
- When you use pictures of people to communicate, show one of the seven basic emotions to get the feeling across clearly.
- People read these emotions fairly well from photos, so use images where the expressions look genuine, since people often spot fake emotion.
- Decide which emotions drive your audience, documenting their psychographics alongside basic demographics, such as what emotions motivate different parts of the audience.

### 73. Emotions are tied to muscle movement and vice versa
Facial muscle movement and emotion are linked in both directions, so blocking expressions dampens feeling, and watching someone's emotion activates the matching regions in the observer's brain.
**Design takeaways:**
- Consider the emotions your product generates, since a sad story that makes someone frown may carry that mood into their next action.
- Watch for unintended expressions that change how people feel, such as squinting and frowning at tiny text, which can block a happy feeling and affect what people do next.
- This is another case for video, because people mimic the expressions they see, so a happy, smiling person on screen tends to make viewers smile, feel happier, and possibly change their next action.

### 74. Anecdotes persuade more than data
Anecdotes come in story form and trigger empathy and emotion, which makes them more persuasive and memorable than raw data.
**Design takeaways:**
- Information is processed more deeply and remembered longer when it has an emotional hook.
- Look for ways to deliver a message that stirs emotion and empathy.
- Use anecdotes alongside or in place of factual data.

### 75. Smells evoke emotions and memories
Smell is the one sense that skips the brain's relay station and goes straight to the emotion and memory centers, so scents trigger strong feelings and memories.
**Design takeaways:**
- Stores, hotels, malls, and similar spaces use scent to call up particular feelings, memories, and associations.
- Scent has been tested in cinemas, and a few studies look at pairing it with online learning.
- Looking ahead, crafting scent for emotional impact may become part of the toolkit of some experience designers.

### 76. People are programmed to enjoy surprises
The brain not only scans for the unexpected but actually craves it, and novelty and surprise light up the pleasure centers more than a preferred reward itself.
**Design takeaways:**
- New and novel things capture attention.
- Something unexpected not only grabs attention but can be genuinely pleasurable.
- Some consistency helps when people are completing a task, but novel and unexpected content is useful when you want people to try something new or come back to see what has changed.

### 77. People are happier when they are busy
People avoid effort yet are happier when they have something to do, and they will choose activity over idleness as long as the task seems worthwhile.
**Design takeaways:**
- People do not like to be idle.
- People will take on a task rather than sit idle, but only if it feels worthwhile, since they would rather stay idle than do busywork.
- People who are busy are happier.
- If a task makes people wait, give them something interesting to do during the wait.

### 78. Pastoral scenes make people happy
People are drawn to pastoral landscape scenes, likely because of evolution, and real views of nature can restore attention and reduce stress.
**Design takeaways:**
- People like pastoral scenes, so when choosing a nature image for a site, pick one with pastoral elements.
- A pastoral scene online draws people in and lifts their mood, yet it will not deliver the health benefits of a real view out a window or a walk outdoors.

### 79. People use look and feel as their first indicator of trust
People first reject a site as untrustworthy based mostly on design factors, and only then judge trustworthiness by content and credibility.
**Design takeaways:**
- People decide quickly what is not trustworthy, rejecting a site first and only then deciding whether to trust it.
- Visual elements like color, font, layout, and navigation decide whether a site clears that first round of trust rejection.
- Once a site clears that first cut, content and credibility determine whether the person actually trusts it.

### 80. Listening to music releases dopamine in the brain
Listening to pleasurable music, and even anticipating it, releases dopamine, and musical taste is highly individual.
**Design takeaways:**
- Music can bring intense pleasure.
- People each have favorite songs that spark euphoria.
- Music is very individual, so what gives one person euphoria may do nothing for another.
- Anticipating the pleasurable parts of music engages different brain areas and neurotransmitters than actually hearing it does.
- Letting people use or add their own music to a site, product, or activity is a powerful way to draw them into a positive and potentially addictive experience.

### 81. The more difficult something is to achieve, the more people like it
When something is hard to join or earn, people value it more, which is explained by cognitive dissonance and by scarcity.
**Design takeaways:**
- This is not a reason to make your product hard to use so people suffer and then like it more, even though that would probably work.
- To build an online community, note that people may use and value it more when joining takes some effort, since an application, criteria, or an invitation can act as a barrier that makes members care more.

### 82. People overestimate reactions to future events
People greatly overestimate how strongly and how long they will react to future good or bad events, while a built-in regulator keeps their happiness fairly steady.
**Design takeaways:**
- Be skeptical when customers claim a particular change will make them much happier or make them quit using something.
- People may prefer one option, or expect to, but their reaction, good or bad, will probably be weaker than they imagine.

### 83. People feel more positive before and after an event than during it
Anticipating an event and remembering it afterward both feel more positive than the event itself.
**Design takeaways:**
- When designing an interface for planning a future event, people feel more positive the longer you can stretch out the planning phase.
- When measuring satisfaction or feelings, expect more positive ratings a few days after the interaction than during it.

### 84. People want what is familiar when they are sad or scared
When sad or scared, people reach for familiar brands and choices, tied to a fear of loss, while they are more willing to try something new when they are happy.
**Design takeaways:**
- Brands are a shortcut, so a brand tied to a past positive experience signals safety to the old brain.
- Brands matter just as much or more online, where, without being able to see or touch the product, the brand stands in for the experience and carries real weight in a purchase.
- Messages of fear or loss may persuade more when your brand is well established, while fun and happy messages may persuade more when your brand is new.

## People Make Mistakes

### 85. People will always make mistakes; there is no fail-safe product
Everyone makes mistakes and no product can be made fully error-proof, so plan for errors and write clear error messages for when they happen.
**Design takeaways:**
- Think ahead about likely mistakes, learn as much as you can about the errors people will make, and change the design before release so those mistakes are avoided.
- Prototype the design and watch real users to see likely errors, testing with the same kind of people who will actually use it rather than your fellow designers.
- Write error messages in plain language that say what the person did, explain the problem, tell how to fix it, use active voice, and show an example.

### 86. People make errors when they are under stress
A little arousal sharpens performance, but too much stress degrades it (the Yerkes-Dodson law) and can trigger tunnel action, where a person repeats the same failing step.
**Design takeaways:**
- For a boring task, raise the arousal level with sound, color, or movement.
- For a difficult task, lower arousal by removing distractions like color, sound, or movement unless they relate directly to the task.
- Under stress, people miss things on the screen and tend to repeat the same actions even when they do not work.
- Do research to find which situations are stressful, using site visits, observation, and interviews, and redesign where stress is present.
- When someone is an expert at a well-learned task, the stress of high stakes can actually cause errors.

### 87. Not all mistakes are bad
Errors can have positive, negative, or neutral consequences, and some errors teach people useful information.
**Design takeaways:**
- While you do not want many errors, errors will happen anyway.
- Since errors are inevitable, look for and record them during user testing, noting whether each consequence is positive, negative, or neutral.
- During and even before user testing, focus first on redesigning to reduce or avoid the errors with negative consequences.

### 88. People make predictable types of errors
Errors fall into predictable categories, including performance errors (commission, omission, and wrong-action) and motor-control errors.
**Design takeaways:**
- People make different types of errors while learning and using your product, so before testing decide which possible errors concern you most.
- During testing and observation, record which category of errors people make, which focuses your redesign.
- When errors can cause accidents or loss of life, use a formal system such as HFACS to analyze and prevent them.

### 89. People use different error strategies
People use different strategies to fix an error, such as systematic exploration, trial and error, or rigidly repeating the same action, and strategy depends more on experience than on age.
**Design takeaways:**
- People use different error-correction strategies, so record which ones your audience uses during testing to predict future issues and guide redesign.
- Do not assume older people cannot finish a task, because they may do it differently and more slowly yet still complete as many tasks as younger people.
- Beyond age, consider novices versus experts, since an older person can be a long-time expert and a younger person can have little experience with a given product.

## How People Decide

### 90. People make most decisions unconsciously
Most decisions are made unconsciously, shaped by factors like what others choose, a person's self-image, reciprocity, and fear of loss, and unconscious does not mean irrational.
**Design takeaways:**
- To design something that persuades people to act, learn the unconscious motivations of your audience.
- Be skeptical when people explain why they decided something, because the decision was unconscious and they may not know the real reasons.
- Even though decisions rest on unconscious factors, people still want rational reasons, so provide clear logical reasons even though they are probably not the true cause.

### 91. The unconscious knows first
The unconscious mind detects danger and sorts good choices from bad ones before the conscious mind becomes aware of it.
**Design takeaways:**
- People respond to unconscious signals of danger.
- The unconscious acts faster than the conscious mind, so people often act or have preferences they cannot explain.

### 92. People want more choices and information than they can process
People say they want many choices, but too many choices overwhelm the thought process and reduce how often people actually choose.
**Design takeaways:**
- Resist the urge to give customers a huge number of choices.
- Ask people how many choices they want and they will nearly always answer "plenty," so be ready to set that reply aside.
- Where possible, keep choices to three or four, and when you must offer more, do it progressively, letting people pick from a few and then from a subset.

### 93. People think choice equals control
People equate having choices with having control and will want choices even when the extra options slow them down.
**Design takeaways:**
- People need to feel in control and to have choices.
- People do not always pick the fastest path, so you may want to offer more than one way to do a task, even if some ways are less efficient, just so people feel they have a choice.
- Once you give people choices, taking them away makes people unhappy, so when a new version improves a method, consider keeping some older methods so people still feel they have options.

### 94. People may care about time more than they care about money
References to time create a personal connection and drive more buying and satisfaction than references to money for most people.
**Design takeaways:**
- Know your market, and if they are driven by prestige and possessions, go ahead and mention money.
- Remember that most people, most of the time, are moved more by time and experiences that build a personal connection than by money or possessions.
- If you cannot afford to study your audience closely and you sell everyday items rather than prestige ones, lean on time and experiences and put off any mention of money.

### 95. Mood influences the decision-making process
People lean toward either intuitive or deliberate decisions, and their mood, along with whether the approach matches their style, affects how much they value a product.
**Design takeaways:**
- Some people decide intuitively and others deliberately.
- People value a product more highly when they can decide in their natural style.
- If you can learn someone's style, suggesting how they decide will raise their estimate of a product's value.
- A person's mood is easy to nudge, for instance with a brief video clip.
- People in a good mood rate a product higher when asked to decide quickly on first feelings.
- People in a sad mood rate a product higher when asked to decide in a more deliberate way.
- By influencing mood, you can steer how people approach the decision, which raises their estimate of value.

### 96. Group decision making can be faulty
Groups that open by sharing their initial preferences review the information poorly and decide worse, though two competent people who discuss freely can beat one.
**Design takeaways:**
- When one member is less competent and does not realize it while the others do, the group tends to decide poorly because it fails to discount that member's opinions.
- Give people a way and the time to weigh all the relevant information on their own before they see what others think.
- Have each person rate their own confidence in a decision before sharing it.
- Once sharing begins, allow enough time for people to talk through their disagreements.
- Because information now spreads easily and widely, this free flow of opinions may lead people to make collectively worse decisions.

### 97. People are swayed by a dominant personality
Dominant people tend to become group leaders and to speak first, and groups often adopt the first answer that is proposed.
**Design takeaways:**
- When designing as a group, be careful not to follow the first solution just because it came first.
- For group meetings, have each member write ideas down ahead of time and circulate them before the meeting.

### 98. When people are uncertain, they let others decide what to do
When unsure, people look to the behavior and reviews of others (social validation) to decide what to do, even trusting total strangers.
**Design takeaways:**
- People are strongly influenced by others' opinions and actions, particularly when they feel unsure.
- Add testimonials, ratings, and reviews when you want to sway behavior.
- The more information a rating or review gives about the person who left it, the more influential it will be.

### 99. People think others are more easily influenced than they are themselves
People believe others are swayed by persuasion while they themselves are not (the third-person effect), a belief that research shows is false.
**Design takeaways:**
- Everyone is affected by unconscious processes.
- In customer research, when people claim that ratings and reviews do not influence them, do not believe it, because these processes are unconscious and people are largely unaware of what affects them.

### 100. People value a product more highly when it is physically in front of them
People will pay more for a product that is physically present and within reach than for one shown as text, a picture, a sample, or kept behind a barrier.
**Design takeaways:**
- Physical stores may keep an edge when they have products on hand, especially on price.
- Putting a product behind glass or another barrier can lower the price a customer is willing to pay.
